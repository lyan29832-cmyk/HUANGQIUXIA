## Technology-transfer lifecycle

### 1. Frame the transfer

Establish the transfer type, objective, products and presentations, processes and methods, sending and receiving units, markets, lifecycle stage, regulatory commitments, supply constraints, and expected end state. Determine whether the work is a site transfer, scale transfer, internal or external transfer, transfer between development and commercial operations, analytical transfer, or a combination.

### 2. Establish governance

Identify the accountable sponsor and decision forum. Define responsibilities for the sending unit, receiving unit, Quality, Regulatory/CMC, manufacturing, QC, engineering, validation, statistics, procurement and supply, IT/data integrity, EHS, and external parties as applicable. Define escalation paths, change-control interfaces, issue management, meeting cadence, records, and approval gates. Use the company's actual governance documents rather than assuming generic titles or authorities.

### 3. Perform capability and gap assessment

Evaluate the receiving unit against the intended product, process, method, and control strategy. Cover at least:

- personnel competence, staffing, training, and access to subject-matter expertise;
- facility, environmental, utility, equipment, automation, and scale differences;
- raw materials, components, suppliers, logistics, storage, and distribution constraints;
- manufacturing instructions, hold times, sampling, in-process controls, and contamination/cross-contamination controls;
- analytical methods, reference standards, instruments, laboratory systems, specifications, stability, and sample flows;
- validation/qualification status, computerized systems, data flows, audit trails, and record retention;
- deviations, OOS/OOT, complaints, recalls, prior changes, CAPA, process trends, and known failure modes;
- registration commitments, approved dossier details, local licences, inspection status, and required submissions or notifications.

For each material gap, document the evidence, impact, risk, action, owner, due date, dependency, and stage-gate consequence.

### 4. Build the knowledge package

Use a structured package that makes the product and process rationale usable by the receiving unit. Depending on scope, include:

- development history, QTPP, CQAs, critical or otherwise relevant material attributes and process parameters;
- process maps, mass balance or flow understanding, scale-up rationale, design studies, control strategy, normal operating ranges, proven or studied ranges, and key assumptions;
- raw-material and component functionality, supplier knowledge, equipment characteristics, facility/utility requirements, automation recipes, alarms, and data dependencies;
- master production and packaging information, sampling plans, specifications, analytical methods, stability strategy, cleaning approach, hold times, and validation history;
- known variability, atypical events, failed or aborted work, deviations, OOS/OOT, changes, CAPA, complaints, and lessons learned;
- regulatory commitments, post-approval obligations, market-specific controls, and approved change pathways;
- tacit-knowledge transfer through observation, demonstrations, joint execution, coaching, questioning, and competency confirmation.

Do not treat volume of documents as a measure of transfer effectiveness. Verify that the receiving unit understands the rationale, can identify uncertainty, and can execute and control the process.

### 5. Plan and execute the transfer

The transfer plan or protocol should define scope, governance, prerequisites, deliverables, stage gates, methods, training, batches or studies, sampling and data strategy, predefined success or acceptance criteria, deviation handling, change-control and regulatory interfaces, escalation, reporting, and closure. Conduct engineering, scale-up, demonstration, analytical, or comparability work as justified by the transfer type and risk.

### 6. Accept, close, and sustain

Transfer acceptance should be based on objective evidence that prerequisites and success criteria were met, material issues were resolved or formally accepted with justified controls, responsibilities were handed over, and required regulatory or quality actions were completed. Define enhanced post-transfer monitoring when warranted. Feed knowledge into PPQ, CPV, PQR/APR, change control, training, supplier oversight, and future transfers.

## Suggested stage gates and minimum evidence

| Gate | Decision question | Minimum evidence to consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initiation | Is the transfer defined, governed, and legally feasible? | Approved scope; product/site/market map; governance; preliminary regulatory, supply, quality, and EHS assessment; high-level plan. |
| Knowledge-transfer readiness | Is the source knowledge complete enough to support a reliable gap assessment and plan? | Knowledge inventory; source-document status; known uncertainties and history; initial receiving-unit gap/risk assessment; information-transfer plan. |
| Execution readiness | Are the receiving unit and transfer controls ready for planned studies or batches? | Closed or controlled critical gaps; qualified facilities/equipment/utilities as applicable; trained personnel; material and method readiness; approved protocol; data and deviation controls. |
| PPQ readiness | Is the process sufficiently defined and controlled for PPQ under intended commercial conditions? | Mature process/control strategy; relevant qualification and method status; approved PPQ protocol and statistical rationale; material/supplier readiness; trained staff; open-item assessment; regulatory alignment. |
| Transfer acceptance | Has the receiving unit demonstrated the intended capability and met predefined transfer criteria? | Executed records; data evaluation; deviations and changes assessed; criteria disposition; knowledge and document handover; approvals; residual-risk decision. |
| Closure and sustainment | Is ownership transferred and ongoing performance controlled? | Closure report; remaining actions with governance; CPV/enhanced monitoring plan; PQR/APR and change-control integration; lessons learned; knowledge-retention arrangements. |

Stage gates are professional governance tools, not universal regulatory clauses. Tailor them to risk, transfer type, company procedure, and governing requirements.

## Process-validation lifecycle

### Stage 1: process design

Build and document process understanding sufficient to define a reproducible commercial process and control strategy. Consider the QTPP, CQAs, input-material attributes, process parameters, unit operations, scale and equipment effects, variability, development and scale-up studies, risk assessments, analytical capability, and data supporting proposed controls. Capture uncertainties and knowledge gaps rather than converting assumptions into facts.

Technology transfer should make Stage 1 knowledge usable at the commercial receiving unit. If substantial development is still occurring during PPQ, explicitly assess whether the process is ready for Stage 2 or whether additional design work is needed.

### Stage 2: process qualification and PPQ

Stage 2 generally combines readiness of facilities, utilities, equipment, systems, materials, methods, personnel, and documentation with PPQ evidence that the commercial process can perform as intended.

Before PPQ, confirm as applicable:

- the process and control strategy are defined and approved for the intended commercial configuration;
- relevant facility, utility, equipment, automation, cleaning, laboratory, and computerized-system qualification/validation is complete or has a justified status;
- raw materials, components, suppliers, master documents, methods, specifications, sampling, stability, and training are ready;
- development and transfer risks, open deviations, changes, atypical results, and unresolved gaps have documented dispositions;
- PPQ objectives, design, commercial operating conditions, number of lots or batches, sampling locations/frequency, statistical methods, predefined criteria, deviation rules, responsibilities, and report requirements are justified prospectively;
- regulatory filing, approval, notification, commitment, and inspection-readiness implications have been assessed.

PPQ design must be science-, risk-, and data-based. The number of lots or batches and samples should reflect process knowledge, variability, complexity, prior experience, scale-up or site differences, detectability, and applicable regional expectations. Do not default to three batches or a fixed sample size without a documented rationale.

PPQ execution should reflect intended routine commercial conditions unless a justified design intentionally challenges or brackets conditions. Record actual conditions and departures. Evaluate within-batch and between-batch variation where relevant. Predefine how protocol deviations, atypical results, OOS/OOT results, missing data, reprocessing/rework, or equipment interruptions will be assessed; none should be ignored or automatically treated as invalidating the entire PPQ without scientific evaluation.

The PPQ report should reconcile execution against the approved protocol; present data and statistical/scientific evaluation; assess deviations, changes, missing data, and criteria; state whether objectives were met; identify residual uncertainty and actions; document the validation decision; and authorize or condition the transition to routine manufacture and Stage 3 as applicable.

### Stage 3: continued process verification

CPV should maintain evidence that the process remains in a state of control during commercial manufacture. Define:

- products, processes, units, sites, data sources, parameters, attributes, and contextual variables in scope;
- data ownership, acquisition, review, integrity, traceability, aggregation, stratification, retention, and access;
- scientific/statistical methods, baselines, review frequency, signal rules, alert/action or escalation logic where justified, and treatment of insufficient data;
- review of variability, trends, shifts, special causes, capability or performance, deviations, OOS/OOT, complaints, yields, stability, equipment/utilities, materials/suppliers, and changes as applicable;
- signal triage, investigation, impact assessment, change control, CAPA, regulatory assessment, management escalation, and effectiveness monitoring;
- interfaces with batch review/release, PQR/APR, quality management review, validation maintenance, knowledge management, and process improvement.

CPV is not a periodic charting exercise. Use signals and accumulated knowledge to support timely decisions. Enhanced monitoring may be appropriate after initial PPQ, transfer, scale or site changes, adverse trends, significant deviations, or other increases in uncertainty.

## Intermediate/bulk hold time within transfer and process validation

When transfer, scale-up, site change, PPQ, or CPV changes the elapsed time between unit operations or the conditions under which an intermediate/bulk product is stored, also use [intermediate-product-hold-time-regulatory-map.md](intermediate-product-hold-time-regulatory-map.md) and [intermediate-product-hold-time-study-framework.md](intermediate-product-hold-time-study-framework.md).

Treat hold-time knowledge as part of the process control strategy, not as a detached stability exercise. During transfer or PPQ:

- inventory each hold point and the exact start/stop event;
- compare sending- and receiving-unit routine/max times, environmental conditions, temporary containers/liners, fill level/headspace, transfer/transport, and restart practices;
- identify cumulative holds and whether changed scheduling, equipment throughput, campaign size, or testing/release delays increase exposure;
- determine whether existing development/hold-time evidence remains representative of the receiving site and commercial configuration;
- challenge the intended maximum hold in PPQ only when the protocol is designed to do so and the challenge is scientifically and operationally representative; otherwise use a dedicated hold-time study or other justified evidence route;
- link intermediate trends to downstream processability and finished-product CQAs, including stability where relevant;
- include hold-time deviations/signals in CPV/PQR review and reassess after changes that alter the study basis.

Do not infer that `three successful PPQ batches` validate every hold time. The hold must be explicitly challenged, separately supported, or otherwise justified under the applicable market framework.

## Statistical and QRM guardrails

- Define the decision the analysis must support before selecting a tool, model, statistic, chart, limit, or sample size.
- Evaluate data provenance, representativeness, independence, measurement-system fitness, missing/excluded data, stratification, within- and between-batch variation, distributional assumptions, and uncertainty.
- Do not claim process capability or stable control from inadequate, selectively pooled, or nonrepresentative data. State limitations when the available number of batches or observations is small.
- Predefine PPQ success criteria and the handling of deviations or missing data. Routine release specifications alone may be insufficient to demonstrate process understanding or control.
- Use bracketing, matrixing, worst-case selection, prior knowledge, platform knowledge, or reduced testing only with documented scientific and risk justification and within applicable regulatory commitments.
- Match QRM formality to uncertainty, importance, and complexity. Record assumptions, subjectivity, evidence strength, residual risk, review triggers, and the effect of new knowledge.
- Avoid using a risk score as the sole basis for a validation or transfer decision. Explain the scientific and patient/product-quality rationale.

## Review patterns

For a technology-transfer plan, protocol, or report, review at least:

1. scope, transfer type, products, sites, markets, and lifecycle stage;
2. governance, sending/receiving-unit responsibilities, MAH/sponsor oversight, and external-party interfaces;
3. knowledge inventory, receiving-unit gap assessment, uncertainties, and known history;
4. change control, regulatory strategy, quality agreement, validation/qualification, and supply dependencies;
5. training and tacit-knowledge transfer;
6. execution design, data and sampling strategy, predefined criteria, deviations, and escalation;
7. acceptance, residual risk, closure, handover, enhanced monitoring, and CPV/PQR integration.

For a PPQ protocol or report, review at least:

1. process and product scope, commercial configuration, and connection to Stage 1/transfer knowledge;
2. readiness evidence for facilities, utilities, equipment, systems, methods, materials, personnel, and documents;
3. rationale for batches/lots, sampling, conditions, statistical methods, criteria, and challenge/bracketing decisions;
4. data integrity, traceability, deviations, atypical results, OOS/OOT, missing data, and protocol changes;
5. scientific/statistical results, variability, criteria disposition, residual uncertainty, conclusion, and CPV transition.

Present findings in an actionable issue log: location; observed text or gap; evidence/rationale; quality, patient, supply, or compliance impact; recommended revision/action; fact requiring confirmation.

## Red flags

Escalate or clearly flag:

- a document dump with no demonstration of understanding or tacit-knowledge transfer;
- no receiving-unit capability/gap assessment, or critical gaps deferred without risk control;
- unclear responsibility between MAH/sponsor, sending unit, receiving unit, and contractor;
- an analytical method called “transferred” without predefined strategy, data, criteria, deviation handling, and approval;
- PPQ begun while material process development or critical readiness gaps remain unresolved;
- “three batches” or another fixed design asserted without product/process-specific rationale;
- PPQ performed under non-routine conditions with no justification or linkage to the intended commercial process;
- acceptance based only on passing release specifications, without evaluation of process variability or protocol objectives;
- weak traceability from samples and raw data to calculations, exclusions, deviations, and conclusions;
- no defined CPV plan or no mechanism to act on process signals;
- outsourced responsibilities, data access, change notification, release interfaces, or knowledge ownership left vague;
- historical regulatory statements from the 2014 publication presented as current requirements;
- AI or advanced analytics used for GxP decisions without defined intended use, data governance, validation/fitness, traceability, change control, monitoring, and accountable review.
