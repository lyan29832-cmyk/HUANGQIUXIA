# ICH Q9(R1) quality risk management

The current ICH-hosted text checked on 2026-09-29 includes minor typographical corrections dated 26 January 2023 and 15 January 2025. ## Jurisdictional holder-risk boundary

ICH Q9(R1) governs pharmaceutical quality risk management; it does not by itself create every jurisdiction-specific duty of a marketing authorisation holder, applicant, application holder, or manufacturer. When the task concerns lifecycle holder governance, post-marketing risk-management plans, benefit-risk management, outsourcing oversight, holder-level periodic risk review, or the allocation of risk responsibilities across China, the EU, the US, and PIC/S environments, also read [mah-holder-risk-management-regulatory-map.md](mah-holder-risk-management-regulatory-map.md).

Keep three layers separate unless the applicable source expressly combines them:

- statutory or regulatory holder/applicant duties;
- pharmacovigilance or product benefit-risk systems such as the EU RMP/RMS or an FDA-required REMS; and
- pharmaceutical quality risk management embedded in the PQS/GMP system.

Do not infer a universal US MAH/RMP obligation from EU or China concepts, and do not present PIC/S as an independent MAH legal regime.

## Hold-time QRM boundary

For intermediate/bulk product hold-time decisions, Q9(R1) can structure the assessment of degradation, physical change, microbiological growth, processability, container/headspace, environmental, transport and cumulative-exposure risks, but QRM cannot replace an explicit jurisdictional study or dossier requirement. Use [intermediate-product-hold-time-regulatory-map.md](intermediate-product-hold-time-regulatory-map.md) for China/EU/EAEU/PIC/S/FDA routing and [intermediate-product-hold-time-study-framework.md](intermediate-product-hold-time-study-framework.md) for implementation. Do not use a low risk score by itself to waive a study required by the applicable product-scope guidance.

## Regulatory baseline

Keep the following distinctions explicit.

### Regulatory text / ICH position

- Quality risk management (QRM) is a systematic process for the assessment, control, communication, and review of risks to medicinal-product quality across the product lifecycle.
- The evaluation of quality risk should be based on scientific knowledge and ultimately linked to protection of the patient. In Q9(R1), risk to quality includes situations in which product availability may be affected and patient harm may result.
- The level of effort, formality, and documentation should be commensurate with the level of risk.
- QRM may support compliance and better decisions but does not remove the obligation to comply with applicable regulatory requirements and must not be used to justify an otherwise unacceptable practice.

### Regulatory interpretation

Treat QRM as a decision-support framework embedded in the pharmaceutical quality system, not as a stand-alone scoring exercise. The process must remain traceable from the risk question through evidence, assessment, control, acceptance, communication, and review.

### Professional assessment

A well-designed risk matrix, FMEA, or other tool can support QRM, but no tool compensates for a poorly framed risk question, weak data, unmanaged uncertainty, biased scoring, or an unsupported acceptance decision.

## Core terminology

Use ICH Q9(R1) terminology unless a jurisdiction-specific controlled term is required.

- **Hazard:** potential source of harm.
- **Harm:** damage to health, including damage that can occur from loss of product quality or availability.
- **Risk:** combination of the probability of occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm.
- **Hazard identification:** systematic use of information to identify potential sources of harm referring to the risk question or problem description.
- **Risk analysis:** estimation of risk associated with identified hazards.
- **Risk evaluation:** comparison of estimated risk against given risk criteria to determine significance.
- **Risk control:** actions implementing risk-management decisions, including risk reduction and/or risk acceptance.
- **Risk acceptance:** informed decision to take a particular risk.
- **Risk review:** review or monitoring of QRM outputs/results considering, when appropriate, new knowledge and experience.
- **Detectability:** ability to discover or determine the existence, presence, or fact of a hazard.
- **Risk-based decision-making:** decision-making that considers knowledge about risks relevant to the decision and whether risks are at an acceptable level.
- **Uncertainty:** in QRM, lack of knowledge about hazards, harms, and consequently their associated risks; it can arise from incomplete knowledge and expected or unexpected variability.

Do not use `risk identification` as a substitute for `hazard identification` when specifically describing the Q9(R1) risk-assessment step. Broader business-risk language may use different terminology; label that framework separately.

## QRM lifecycle workflow

### 1. Initiate and frame

Define:
- the problem and/or risk question;
- the decision that the assessment must support;
- product/process/system and lifecycle scope;
- assumptions, boundaries, interfaces, and exclusions;
- available background information and data;
- leader, participants, decision maker(s), resources, timeline, and deliverables;
- the intended level of effort, formality, and documentation.

A risk question should be specific enough that the assessment can answer it. Avoid vague prompts such as `assess all risks associated with this change` without defining the decision, boundary, and consequence of interest.

### 2. Risk assessment

Use the three fundamental questions as a baseline:
1. What might go wrong?
2. What is the likelihood/probability that it will go wrong?
3. What are the consequences/severity?

Then:
- identify hazards systematically;
- analyze likelihood/probability and severity;
- include detectability only when relevant to the chosen method and scientifically supportable;
- evaluate the analyzed risk against predefined or otherwise justified risk criteria;
- assess the strength of evidence, robustness of the data set, assumptions, uncertainty, and data limitations.

Risk output can be qualitative or quantitative. Qualitative descriptors such as high/medium/low should be defined as clearly as practicable. A numerical score is not automatically a quantitative probability estimate.

### 3. Risk control

Determine:
- whether the risk is above an acceptable level;
- what can reduce or eliminate the risk;
- the appropriate balance among benefits, risks, and resources;
- whether controls introduce new risks or increase other risks.

Risk reduction can act on severity, probability of harm, and, when appropriate, detection controls. Reassess after implementation when controls may change the risk profile. Record the residual risk and the basis for accepting or further reducing it.

### 4. Risk communication

Communicate and document QRM outputs/results to relevant decision makers and stakeholders at the appropriate points in the process. Tailor the detail and route to the importance and audience of the decision.

### 5. Risk review

Treat QRM as ongoing. Implement a mechanism to review or monitor events and reconsider the original decision when new knowledge or experience becomes relevant.

Planned review inputs may include:
- product quality review or equivalent lifecycle review;
- inspections and audits;
- change control;
- validation or qualification results;
- stability, process capability, or continued process verification data;
- supplier and outsourced-activity performance.

Unplanned review inputs may include:
- deviations and failure investigations;
- OOS/OOT or significant adverse trends;
- complaints and recalls;
- unexpected process, facility, equipment, utility, or supply-chain events.

Set review frequency proportionate to risk. Reconsider risk acceptance when review evidence changes the original basis.

## Q9(R1) emphasis: formality

Formality is a continuum, not a binary formal/informal choice. Determine the rigor and formality using available knowledge and, especially:

- **Uncertainty:** more uncertainty can require greater formality and knowledge-generation effort.
- **Importance:** more important decisions for product quality generally justify more formal treatment and stronger reduction of uncertainty.
- **Complexity:** more complex systems or questions generally justify greater formality.

Describe within the quality system how the organization determines the appropriate degree of formality. Do not use lack of time, people, or other resource constraints as the reason for reducing formality. Regardless of formality, risk scores, ratings, and assessments should be grounded in evidence, science, and knowledge.

Characteristics that may support higher formality include explicit performance of all QRM elements, a stand-alone QRM document, use of a suitable tool, a cross-functional team, and an experienced facilitator. Lower-formality decisions can embed QRM within another quality-system process and may not require a stand-alone tool or report, provided the resulting risk decision remains robust and appropriately documented.

Do not infer that `high risk = FMEA required`. The formality decision and tool choice are separate.

## Q9(R1) emphasis: risk-based decision-making

Risk-based decision-making is inherent in QRM. For each material decision, make the following traceable:
- the decision question and alternatives;
- knowledge and evidence used;
- hazards and risk implications of the alternatives;
- required controls;
- acceptability of residual risk;
- affected stakeholders;
- monitoring/review plan.

Use more structured comparison of alternatives when importance, uncertainty, or complexity warrants it. Less structured approaches can be appropriate when existing knowledge is strong and uncertainty/complexity is lower. Rule-based decisions can be appropriate when an established SOP, policy, specification, or well-understood requirement already encodes a prior risk-based decision.

Preserve data integrity in the evidence used for risk-based decisions.

## Q9(R1) emphasis: managing and minimizing subjectivity

Assume that subjectivity can enter every QRM stage, particularly hazard identification, estimation of probability and severity, estimation of risk reduction, and interpretation of acceptability.

Control subjectivity by:
- defining the risk question precisely;
- identifying assumptions explicitly;
- using relevant data and knowledge before expert opinion alone;
- defining rating scales and decision criteria before scoring where practicable;
- anchoring scale levels to observable evidence or context-specific examples;
- using an appropriate cross-functional team for consequential or uncertain decisions;
- documenting disagreement and the rationale for the final decision;
- challenging cognitive bias, anchoring, availability effects, familiarity effects, confirmation bias, and escalation of commitment when they could affect the conclusion;
- using an experienced facilitator or independent challenge when the importance or uncertainty warrants it;
- performing sensitivity or scenario analysis when small rating changes could reverse the decision.

Do not claim that subjectivity can be eliminated. The Q9(R1) objective is to manage and minimize it.

## Risk scoring, matrices, FMEA, and detectability

### Regulatory interpretation

Q9(R1) permits qualitative descriptions, quantitative estimates, and relative risk measures. It does not prescribe a universal 1-5 or 1-10 scoring scale, RPN formula, traffic-light matrix, or acceptance threshold.

Detectability can factor into some methods, but it is not necessarily a discrete factor in every QRM method. Detection controls may be part of risk reduction; this does not mean a weak or late detection control should automatically offset a severe hazard.

### Professional assessment controls

When using a matrix or FMEA/FMECA:
- define what each severity, occurrence/probability, and detectability level means in the case context;
- document the evidence for each rating;
- state whether the score is ordinal, semiquantitative, or a true quantitative estimate;
- do not treat multiplication of ordinal labels as mathematical precision;
- review high-severity scenarios separately when an aggregate score or RPN could mask them;
- avoid universal RPN cutoffs copied from unrelated processes or legacy templates;
- test whether reasonable rating changes would alter prioritization or acceptance;
- distinguish `existing controls` from `proposed controls` and reassess residual risk after controls;
- identify new/secondary risks introduced by controls.

If an organization uses fixed action thresholds, justify and govern them within the relevant context. Do not present a threshold as an ICH requirement unless the source explicitly states it.

## Select a proportionate method or tool

Q9(R1) Annex I gives examples, not a mandatory tool list. Select the method based on the question and the nature of the system.

| Method | Best-fit use | Key cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic facilitation / flowchart / checklist / structured expert review | Simple or well-understood questions; lower-complexity embedded decisions | Keep the risk question, evidence, decision, and rationale traceable even when no formal tool is used. |
| FMEA / FMECA | Failure modes, effects, existing controls, and prioritized actions | Scoring can become subjective; RPN can hide different severity/occurrence profiles. |
| FTA | Pathways and combinations leading to a defined top event | Requires a well-defined top event and reliable logic/data; can become complex. |
| HACCP | Process hazards and critical-control-point logic | Use only when the hazard/critical-control framing fits the pharmaceutical process. |
| HAZOP | Systematic deviations from intended process/design parameters | Requires suitable guide words, current process knowledge, and competent facilitation. |
| PHA | Early-stage or preliminary hazard screening | Often needs follow-up analysis as knowledge matures. |
| Risk ranking/filtering | Portfolio, site, supplier, audit, or issue prioritization | Criteria and weighting can introduce hidden subjectivity. |
| Statistical tools | Trends, variability, capability, uncertainty, and evidence evaluation | Do not equate statistical significance with clinical or quality significance. |

Combining methods is acceptable when each adds decision-relevant information. Complexity of the tool is not evidence of quality.

## Risk control, residual risk, and secondary risk

For every material control:
- state the mechanism by which it changes severity, probability, detectability, or knowledge/uncertainty;
- identify implementation owner and timing when an action is required;
- define effectiveness evidence;
- reassess the risk after implementation;
- record the residual risk and acceptance decision;
- evaluate whether the control creates a new risk or increases another existing risk.

`Residual risk` and `secondary risk` are useful practical terms. Do not invent a residual-risk acceptance decision when the decision authority or acceptance criteria are not provided.

## Monitoring, trends, and review triggers

### Regulatory baseline

Q9(R1) requires ongoing review/monitoring of QRM outputs and consideration of new knowledge and experience. Review frequency should be based on risk.

### Professional implementation

For persistent or lifecycle risks, consider defined indicators and trigger conditions. Examples include:
- deviation/OOS/OOT frequency or recurrence;
- process capability or control-chart signals;
- critical supplier performance trends;
- complaint or defect trends;
- equipment/utility reliability signals;
- recurring audit findings;
- product availability/supply-risk signals.

Key risk indicators (KRIs), thresholds, dashboards, and risk registers are implementation options, not universal ICH Q9(R1) requirements. If used, link the indicator to the risk, define the trigger, identify the owner, and predefine the response to a threshold breach where practicable.

## Product availability risks

Q9(R1) explicitly includes product-availability consequences where quality/manufacturing issues can lead to patient harm. When applicable, assess:
- process variability and state of control;
- facility/equipment robustness, maintenance, aging, and human-error vulnerability;
- outsourced activities and supplier performance;
- supply-chain dependencies and resilience;
- whether digitalization, automation, isolation technology, or other modern technology reduces or introduces relevant quality/manufacturing risk.

Do not use product-availability concerns to waive GMP or product-quality requirements. Treat availability as an additional patient-protection dimension within the quality risk decision.

## Responsibilities and governance

QRM activities are usually, but not always, undertaken by interdisciplinary teams. Select expertise according to the question rather than using a fixed roster.

Decision makers should have the competence and authority to make timely QRM decisions and should ensure that:
- QRM is coordinated across relevant functions;
- the QRM process is defined, deployed, and reviewed;
- adequate resources and knowledge are available;
- subjectivity is managed and minimized.

For high-consequence decisions, separate facilitation/analysis from final acceptance authority where doing so improves independent challenge. Record who owns the risk, who decides acceptance, and who verifies control effectiveness.

## Minimum QRM record

Unless a lower-formality embedded approach is justified, capture at least:
- title/reference and version;
- risk question/problem statement and decision to be supported;
- scope, boundaries, interfaces, assumptions, exclusions, and lifecycle stage;
- applicable requirements and evidence sources;
- team/roles, facilitator when used, and decision authority;
- method/tool and reason for selection;
- rating scales/criteria and their rationale when scoring is used;
- hazards, potential harms/consequences, causes/failure modes, and existing controls;
- analysis, evidence strength, uncertainty, and data limitations;
- initial risk evaluation;
- proposed controls/actions, owners, and target dates where applicable;
- residual risk and acceptance decision;
- new/secondary risks introduced by controls;
- effectiveness checks;
- communication requirements;
- review frequency and trigger events;
- approvals and revision history as required by the quality system.

For a lower-formality assessment embedded in another quality-system record, keep the same decision logic but scale the documentation to the risk and the formality rationale.

## QRM review / audit checklist

When reviewing an existing QRM document, test each of these questions:

1. Is the risk question specific, decision-oriented, and within a defined boundary?
2. Is patient protection, including relevant product-availability impact, visible in the consequence logic?
3. Are requirements, scientific knowledge, and data distinguished from assumptions and opinion?
4. Does the selected level of formality fit risk, uncertainty, importance, and complexity?
5. Was lower formality chosen for a sound reason rather than resource pressure?
6. Is the tool fit for the question rather than selected by habit?
7. Are hazard, harm, risk, failure mode/cause, and consequence used consistently?
8. Are scales and criteria defined before or independently of the desired outcome?
9. Are ratings evidence-based and is uncertainty acknowledged?
10. Could bias, group dynamics, or an arbitrary scoring formula be driving the result?
11. Does a high-severity risk receive adequate attention even if its aggregate score is modest?
12. Is detectability used only where meaningful and supportable?
13. Are controls preventive/robust where practicable rather than relying only on detection?
14. Is residual risk explicitly reassessed and accepted by the appropriate authority?
15. Were new or secondary risks created by the controls considered?
16. Are communication, effectiveness checks, and review triggers defined?
17. Do later data, deviations, changes, audits, complaints, or trends undermine the original assumptions?
18. Is the final decision traceable, reproducible, and consistent with applicable requirements?

## Output pattern for QRM work

When the user asks for a QRM analysis or review, prefer:

1. **Conclusion** - the decision-relevant finding in concise language.
2. **Scope and assumptions** - what was and was not assessed.
3. **Regulatory text/evidence** - exact governing source, section/page, and currentness status.
4. **Regulatory interpretation** - what the source means for this case.
5. **Professional assessment** - scientific/QA judgment clearly labeled as such.
6. **Issue log** - location, issue, evidence/rationale, impact, proposed revision/action.
7. **Revised QRM structure or risk table** - only using facts actually available.
8. **Residual risk / acceptance / review triggers** - with placeholders when facts or authority are missing.
9. **Second-pass QA** - verify terminology, logic, evidence, scoring, cross-references, and source-to-conclusion consistency.

Never fabricate scores, probabilities, acceptance limits, controls, owners, dates, or risk-acceptance decisions.
