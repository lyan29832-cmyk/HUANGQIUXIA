# Process-validation lifecycle assessment case patterns

## Purpose and source boundary

Use this reference to apply worked-example reasoning from PDA Technical Report No. 60 (Revised 2026), *Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach*, together with relevant durable transfer lessons from Gibson and Schmitt, eds., *Technology and Knowledge Transfer: Keys to Successful Implementation and Management* (copyright 2014). The publications are secondary industry guidance. This file is an original synthesis; it does not reproduce their tables, figures, worked calculations, or case narratives.

Use the synthesized knowledge only within the account for which the source copies were provided. Do not expose the source PDFs or substantial extracts through Skill outputs. Verify current legal and regulatory requirements through official sources for the relevant jurisdiction and execution date.

## How to use a case pattern

1. Define the decision: what must be decided, by whom, at which lifecycle stage, for which product/process/site/market, and by what date.
2. Select the closest case pattern by uncertainty, process characteristics, data structure, scale/site situation, and decision type - not merely by dosage form.
3. Separate transferable reasoning from case-specific facts. Reuse the question sequence, evidence categories, and decision logic; do not copy the original numerical design.
4. Confirm applicability against the actual QTPP, CQAs, material attributes, process parameters, control strategy, development history, dossier commitments, company procedures, and regional requirements.
5. State assumptions and uncertainty. If key evidence is missing, identify the study, monitoring, or governance action needed to close the gap.
6. Label conclusions as `Regulatory text`, `Case-derived assessment pattern`, or `Professional assessment`.

## Full-lifecycle assessment map

| Lifecycle area | Core decision | Evidence to review | Expected output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 - process design | Is the commercial process and control strategy scientifically understood and sufficiently mature? | QTPP/CQAs; product and process development; prior/platform knowledge; material variability; process map; scale-up/model qualification; DoE/characterization; methods; stability; clinical/engineering batches; risks and knowledge gaps. | Process Design/Development Report; updated CQA/parameter assessment; control strategy; remaining studies; PPQ inputs; knowledge record. |
| Stage 1 to Stage 2 transition | Does the available knowledge support commercial-scale qualification, or is more development/engineering work required? | Facility fit; transfer gaps; model applicability; scale/equipment differences; operator experience; data representativeness; open changes/deviations; regulatory alignment. | Stage-gate decision with conditions, owners, due dates, residual risk, and approval. |
| Stage 2 - facilities, utilities, equipment, and systems | Are systems fit for intended use and controlled at a level commensurate with impact? | User requirements; design/function; commissioning; qualification; calibration; maintenance; automation; methods; data systems; risk assessment; traceability to process requirements. | Qualification strategy and evidence; unresolved-impact assessment; readiness status. |
| Stage 2 - PPQ | Does the integrated commercial process perform reproducibly under intended routine conditions? | PPQ rationale; lots/batches; sampling; normal conditions; planned variability; criteria; statistical methods; deviations; stability; cleaning/hold/transport and other supporting validation as applicable. | Approved protocol; executed evidence; PPQ report; validation decision; Stage 3a plan. |
| Stage 3a - heightened monitoring | What residual uncertainty remains after PPQ, and how much additional evidence is needed before routine monitoring? | PPQ results; within/between-batch variability; method variability; material/parameter/attribute trends; changes; deviations/OOS/OOT; stability; complaints and other signals. | Protocol-driven enhanced monitoring; preliminary baselines; updated control strategy; justified transition criteria for Stage 3b. |
| Stage 3b - continued process verification | Does the process remain in a state of control, and are signals acted upon without delay or overreaction? | Routine process/material/product data; SPC/multivariate analysis; yields; deviations; OOS/OOT; complaints; stability; suppliers; equipment/utilities; changes; multiple-site data where relevant. | Periodic or continuous CPV assessment; signal decisions; investigations/change/CAPA; PQR/APR inputs; knowledge and control-strategy updates. |
| Lifecycle change or transfer | What previously established knowledge remains applicable, and what must be re-established after the change? | Pre/post-change data; established conditions/commitments; transfer gap/risk; equipment/scale/site/supplier/personnel differences; comparability; PPQ/CPV history. | Change-specific validation strategy; regulatory assessment; enhanced monitoring; updated validation/knowledge records. |
| Legacy process | Can the product enter routine Stage 3b, does it need a Stage 3a-like evidence phase, or must it return to Stage 1? | Historical validation; batch/process trends; PQR/APR; deviations/OOS/OOT; complaints/recalls/pharmacovigilance as relevant; changes; material/equipment/method history; current control strategy and compliance. | One of three justified paths: routine CPV, heightened data collection, or renewed development/process-design work. |

## Case-derived assessment patterns

### A. Knowledge, design, and transfer patterns

| Situation reflected in the examples | Reusable assessment logic | Appropriate response | Do not transplant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A platform or similar process is proposed as prior knowledge | Demonstrate similarity of molecule/product sensitivity, unit operations, equipment, materials, controls, scale, methods, and failure modes. Identify where the new product breaks the platform assumptions. | Create an applicability matrix linking each reused dataset to the new decision; require new studies for unsupported differences. | A statement that the same platform or equipment is used is not sufficient evidence. |
| First-in-class product, new site/equipment, or limited Stage 1 data | Treat low knowledge as increased uncertainty. Determine whether additional characterization, engineering/demonstration batches, qualification, or enhanced PPQ/CPV evidence is needed. | Use an evidence-gap plan and stage gate before PPQ. Increase effort where it most reduces uncertainty rather than mechanically increasing all testing. | Do not assume that more PPQ batches can compensate for an immature process design. |
| CQA or parameter criticality is uncertain | Assess patient-impact severity separately from knowledge/uncertainty and process occurrence/control. Update the assessment when characterization or clinical/manufacturing evidence changes. | Use the risk assessment to prioritize studies and refine the control strategy; preserve prior and revised rationales. | Avoid permanent binary labels based only on early knowledge or detectability. |
| Scale/site transfer reveals facility-fit differences | Trace process requirements to facility, utility, equipment, automation, material flow, sampling, cleaning, and data-system capabilities. | Close or formally control gaps before the affected gate; qualify new or modified systems and confirm commercial operating ranges. | Do not treat successful source-site operation as proof of receiving-site readiness. |
| A process effect is linked to a quality variant or impurity | Establish the causal pathway, verify it experimentally where feasible, adjust parameter/hold-time controls, and reassess downstream impact. | Update the risk assessment, parameter designation, control strategy, process description, acceptance criteria, and CPV variables. | Do not change only the batch record while leaving the scientific and regulatory knowledge trail unchanged. |
| Raw-material or supplier variability changes process behavior | Identify the relevant material attribute, supplier/lot distribution, process interaction, and impact on CQA or performance. | Add justified material controls, supplier oversight, process controls, studies, or monitoring; reassess across stages. | Meeting a broad incoming specification does not prove functional equivalence. |
| Technology-transfer success is being defined | Assess product quality/comparability, process manufacturability and robustness, and transfer execution/knowledge uptake as distinct dimensions. | Define measurable success criteria before execution and retain lessons learned after closure. | Passing release specifications alone does not demonstrate an effective transfer. |
| Analytical results differ between sites | Investigate method maturity, instrument/reagent/column/filter differences, sample homogeneity/stability/shipping, analyst technique, CDS/LIMS calculations and integration rules, and statistical versus practical significance. | Set method-appropriate criteria prospectively and decide whether the issue is method, sample, site, or true product difference. | Statistical significance alone does not establish a quality-relevant difference; a familiar method is not automatically transferable. |

### B. PPQ design and execution patterns

| Situation reflected in the examples | Reusable assessment logic | Appropriate response | Do not transplant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Selecting the number of PPQ lots/batches | First establish a justified baseline from product/process knowledge; then assess quality/technical controls and residual uncertainty; finally document non-quality constraints separately. | Use a transparent decision tree or checklist and reference supporting data. Increase or reduce the provisional design only with scientific/risk justification and regional acceptability. | Do not adopt three, five, or any other case number as a universal rule. Business need may inform scheduling but cannot override product-quality evidence. |
| Selecting PPQ samples | Link sampling locations, timing, units, replication, test methods, confidence/coverage goals, and analysis to CQA severity, expected variability, data structure, and the decision to be made. | Demonstrate representativeness and the ability to assess relevant within- and between-batch variability. | Do not copy beginning/middle/end counts from an aseptic filling example into another process without confirming the variability structure. |
| Some measurements are below detection or lack unique values | Determine whether the result is censored by LOD/LOQ, rounded, truncated, or genuinely near-constant. Preserve raw instrument precision where justified. | Use suitable censored-data methods, descriptive/graphical evidence, or alternative measures; state why a planned statistic is invalid. | Do not fabricate numerical variation, substitute arbitrary values, or claim capability from an unusable dataset. |
| Repeated samples come from a homogeneous bulk pool | Ask whether multiple samples measure process heterogeneity or mainly analytical/sampling variation. | Focus on appropriate characterization of the pool, upstream sources, method variability, and between-batch reproducibility. | More aliquots from one homogeneous pool do not automatically provide more information about intrabatch process variation. |
| PPQ under normal operating conditions | Use Stage 1 characterization to support NOR/PAR and robustness. Assess whether routine commercial conditions expose expected variability. | Run PPQ under intended routine conditions; use justified supplemental engineering/challenge studies for range extremes. | Do not force PPQ to test every edge of a range when Stage 1 evidence addresses robustness, or claim an untested range solely from nominal PPQ. |
| Bracketing across strengths, sizes, fills, speeds, or batches | Identify a single varying factor and prove selected extremes represent intermediate cases for both process behavior and quality/stability risk. | Document the scientific worst-case rationale and confirm that other variables remain controlled. | Highest/lowest numeric value is not automatically the true worst case. |
| Matrix design across multiple variables | Map all combinations and select tested cells that jointly represent relevant interactions and extremes. | Justify omitted cells, account for stability and product/process interactions, and define when full coverage is required. | Do not use matrixing when untested interactions could materially change risk. |
| Equipment or solution family/grouping | Establish sameness or equivalence in design, materials, operating principle, controls/software, qualification, scale, parameter ranges, and historical performance; identify a representative or worst case. | Qualify the family definition and monitor non-representative members as justified. | Same manufacturer, model name, or formulation label alone does not establish a family. |
| PAT-based, continuous, concurrent, or hybrid validation | Confirm scientific process understanding, model/system fitness, reliable measurements, control algorithms, governance, approved strategy, and applicable regional conditions. | Validate the intended control function and maintain model/instrument performance through lifecycle controls. | Do not confuse continuous process verification with continued process verification or use exceptional concurrent validation for convenience. |

### C. Stage 3, legacy, and change patterns

| Situation reflected in the examples | Reusable assessment logic | Appropriate response | Do not transplant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transition from PPQ to Stage 3a | Use PPQ outcomes and residual uncertainty to determine enhanced variables, sampling/testing, review frequency, signal rules, and duration/exit criteria. | Maintain protocol-driven heightened monitoring until enough representative data support the Stage 3b strategy. | Do not set a fixed number of Stage 3a batches without a statistical and risk basis. |
| Preliminary control limits with limited data | Distinguish provisional baselines from mature limits and reassess them as representative data accumulate. | Use conservative interpretation, qualified statistical support, and planned recalculation/confirmation. | Counts used in a publication's case example for preliminary or final limits are not universal requirements. |
| CPV signal or drift | First determine whether the event is an OOS/OOT/deviation; assess statistical strength and practical relevance; review changes and 6M sources; classify assignable/common/special cause as supportable. | Select proportionate monitoring, investigation, control-strategy review, change control, CAPA, or escalation; document the decision. | Do not overreact to a single false signal or ignore a statistically weak but quality-relevant pattern. |
| Too many sensitizing SPC rules | Balance earlier detection against the increasing false-positive rate and operational burden. | Select rules based on drift types worth detecting, data frequency, process risk, and response capability. | More alarm rules do not necessarily improve process control. |
| Legacy product with incomplete development knowledge | Use historical lifecycle data and a current compliance/control-strategy review to classify the pathway: robust enough for Stage 3b, needs heightened monitoring, or needs Stage 1 work. | Document missing knowledge, risk, data plan, control-strategy changes, and governance. | Do not retrospectively manufacture a QbD narrative unsupported by historical evidence. |
| Change during commercial lifecycle | Define pre-change baseline, changed factors, established conditions/commitments, comparability, residual uncertainty, and post-change monitoring. Consider cumulative incremental changes. | Use CPV to compare performance and determine whether control strategy, validation, registration, or monitoring needs revision. | A successful batch immediately after a change is not by itself evidence of sustained control. |
| Multi-site process monitoring | Harmonize definitions, units, data lineage, methods, limits, contextual variables, and governance before pooling or comparing data. | Use a cross-functional product/process team to assess site-specific and common signals and maintain the knowledge file. | Do not pool site data when methods, scales, controls, or data structures are not comparable. |

## Worked-example lessons by product type

### Large-molecule example

The report's biologics example shows a chain from QTPP and presumptive CQAs, through platform-informed process definition, clinical manufacture, technology transfer, modified FMEA, DoE and qualified scale-down models, to facility-fit assessment, engineering runs, PPQ readiness, PPQ/stability, and CPV governance.

Retain these lessons:

- platform knowledge is useful only after molecule- and site-specific applicability is demonstrated;
- clinical and characterization data should update parameter/CQA decisions and the control strategy;
- a discovered product-variant mechanism can drive tighter hold-time or process controls;
- facility fit may identify new equipment that must be designed, commissioned, qualified, and integrated before PPQ;
- engineering runs can close operational and training gaps but do not automatically count as PPQ;
- product technical teams and maintained specification/process-knowledge files support multi-site Stage 3 decisions.

Do not reuse its specific scales, numbers of runs, stability timing, or control-limit establishment points without independent justification and regulatory assessment.

### Small-molecule parenteral example

The report's parenteral example connects formulation and container sensitivity, DoE, process-temperature/oxygen/pH controls, multiple API suppliers, full-scale process-evaluation batches, risk-based worst-case and matrix choices, facility/equipment qualification, method transfer, demonstration batches, PPQ, hold-time/cleaning/stability work, and CPV.

Retain these lessons:

- formulation route and sterilization strategy must follow product sensitivity and sterility-assurance evidence; terminal sterilization preference and jurisdictional expectations must be assessed independently;
- raw-material particle-size or supplier variability may affect dissolution, mixing, impurity, or other process behavior even when material is within specification;
- strength, concentration, supplier, fill/hold conditions, scale, and container configuration can interact, so worst-case and matrix choices require a causal rationale;
- knowledge from feasibility, formulation, process-evaluation, stability, demonstration, and PPQ batches should be analyzed as a connected evidence set while preserving differences in purpose and GMP status;
- Stage 3 should maintain a living process-understanding record and reassess it after site additions or other changes.

Do not reuse its aseptic sampling model, batch allocation, storage conditions, capability thresholds, or development sequence as a default for other products.

### Technology- and analytical-transfer examples

The older transfer publication provides durable lessons that remain useful when separated from its historical regulatory context:

- technical development and transfer may overlap; stage gates should reveal whether the receiving unit is executing a mature process or continuing development;
- early sourcing and regulatory-strategy decisions can prevent later comparability, analytical, and supply burdens;
- transfer success should include product quality, process performance/manufacturability, and project/knowledge-transfer effectiveness;
- analytical transfer may fail because of method ambiguity, equipment, consumables, sample handling, software/integration, or unsuitable acceptance criteria even when the method performed well at the sending site;
- statistical differences must be interpreted against analytical capability, reporting level, specification, patient/product relevance, and predefined transfer objectives.

Treat any regulatory assertions, named-product facts, or historical three-batch conventions in that publication as non-current until verified.

## Statistical method-selection patterns

Statistical methods answer different questions. Select the method from the decision and data-generating structure; do not choose it because it appeared in a worked example.

| Decision question | Candidate pattern | Inputs and assumptions to verify | Main limitation/guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How many lots on average would be needed to encounter a specified unacceptable lot-failure rate? | Average run length, conceptually `ARL = 1/p` | Meaningful failure definition; approximately stable failure probability; independence. | An average waiting time is not a confidence statement and is not sufficient by itself for PPQ design. |
| How much between-lot variation is expected to be represented by a given number of lots? | Order-statistic/expected-coverage concept | Representative, independent lots and clear distinction between expected coverage and confidence. | Expected coverage can be low with few lots; use a phased PPQ-to-CPV plan where appropriate. |
| Can a defined proportion of results be expected within limits with stated confidence? | Statistical tolerance interval | Distribution or nonparametric basis; method variability; lot hierarchy; confidence and coverage; representative sampling. | Do not pool lots if between-lot differences are material; hierarchical methods may be needed. |
| Are lot means or variances different? | ANOVA plus variance-comparison methods, or suitable robust/nonparametric models | Adequate power; distribution; independence; balanced/unbalanced design; multiple populations. | Failure to detect a difference is not proof of equivalence. Statistical significance must be interpreted for quality relevance. |
| Is the process stable over time and are there special-cause signals? | SPC charts selected for variables/attribute data and subgroup structure | Time order; rational subgrouping; chart assumptions; enough representative periods; prespecified signal rules. | Limits based on sparse PPQ data are provisional; excessive supplementary rules increase false alarms. |
| Is process performance/capability adequate relative to limits? | Pp/Ppk or Cp/Cpk with confidence/uncertainty as appropriate | Statistical control/stability; distribution; centering; within- versus overall variation; measurement fitness; sufficient data. | Do not use a universal index threshold or report optimistic confidence intervals that ignore between-lot variation. |
| Is the lot-conformance rate above a target with stated confidence? | Confidence-for-reliability/binomial pattern | Independent successive lots; unambiguous pass/fail; target conformance and confidence. | The required lots may be impractical for PPQ; a preplanned PPQ-to-CPV evidence continuum may be more appropriate. |
| Can a smaller design gain power by using tighter internal PPQ limits? | Narrow-limit gauging | Scientifically justified inner limits; stable centered process; distinction from release specifications. | Narrow PPQ limits must not replace registered/release acceptance criteria or distort batch disposition. |
| Is between-lot variation acceptably small relative to within-lot variation? | Variance-components model | Replicates within lots; enough lots; model fit; measurement contribution; power for the chosen ratio. | Point estimates from few lots are unstable; quantify uncertainty. |
| How precisely can the standard deviation be estimated? | Precision-based sample-size design | Target relative precision/confidence; independent representative observations; correct variance level. | Variance estimation often requires more data than PPQ can provide; continue planned estimation in CPV. |
| Are lots practically equivalent within a scientifically meaningful margin? | Equivalence testing such as TOST/extensions | Prospective equivalence margin; power; variance model; multiple-lot structure. | A nonsignificant difference test is not equivalence; the margin cannot be selected after seeing results. |

Use a statistician or suitably qualified data-science/subject-matter expert for nontrivial designs. Document raw-data provenance, exclusions, rounding, censored values, units, software/model version, assumptions, sensitivity analysis, and reproducibility.

## Oral solid dosage-form adaptation

The worked product examples in TR 60 (Revised 2026) are a large-molecule process and a small-molecule parenteral process. The following is a professional adaptation for oral solid dosage forms, not a substitute for dosage-form-specific official guidance or PDA TR 60-2.

| Transferable case logic | Oral solid dosage-form application questions |
| --- | --- |
| Material variability | Which API/excipient attributes affect flow, segregation, compressibility, compactability, dissolution, moisture sensitivity, lubrication, or coating? Are supplier/grade/particle-size distributions represented? |
| Process map and CQA linkage | How do dispensing/sieving, granulation, drying, milling, blending, lubrication, compression/encapsulation, coating, and packaging affect assay, content uniformity, dissolution, impurities, hardness/friability, moisture, appearance, and stability? |
| Scale and equipment fit | Are bowl/load, geometry, shear, spray, airflow, heat/mass transfer, screen, feeder, dwell time, compression force, pan, and control-system differences scientifically bridged? |
| Risk-based PPQ sampling | Does the design detect time/location segregation, start-middle-end effects, stratification across containers, compression/coating drift, and relevant within/between-batch variability without introducing sampling bias? |
| Bracketing/matrix/family | Are strengths based on a common blend/granulation or proportional formulation? Do dose, tablet weight, tooling, coating, dissolution, equipment, and packaging differences preserve the assumed worst-case relationship? |
| Hold-time and supporting validation | Are bulk/intermediate holds, dirty/clean holds, environmental exposure, cleaning, transport, packaging moisture/light protection, and method readiness connected to the PPQ and stability strategy? |
| CPV | Are material attributes, blend/granulation/drying/compression/coating parameters, yields, IPCs, assay/CU/dissolution, deviations/OOS/OOT, complaints, stability, and supplier/equipment changes trended with appropriate stratification? |

For an oral solid dosage-form case, explicitly mark which conclusions come from general lifecycle principles and which require product-specific or dosage-form-specific evidence.

## Required case-based output structure

When using these case patterns to assess a real validation problem, produce:

1. `Decision and scope`: product, process/unit operation, site, scale, market, lifecycle stage, and decision owner.
2. `Regulatory basis`: current official requirements and commitments, separately identified.
3. `Known facts and evidence`: source, relevance, representativeness, reliability, and data lineage.
4. `Prior knowledge applicability`: similarities, differences, and unsupported assumptions.
5. `CQA/material/parameter/control linkage`: causal rationale and current control strategy.
6. `Uncertainty and risk`: severity, probability/variation, detectability/control, knowledge gaps, residual risk, and patient/product/supply impact.
7. `Options assessed`: studies, batches, sampling, statistics, controls, monitoring, timing, and trade-offs.
8. `Predefined decision criteria`: scientific, statistical, quality, regulatory, and operational criteria without post-hoc adjustment.
9. `Conclusion`: approved, conditionally approved, not ready, or additional evidence required, with rationale.
10. `Lifecycle follow-through`: actions, owners, due dates, change/regulatory impact, CPV/PQR integration, review trigger, and knowledge-record update.

If case facts are missing, use explicit placeholders or a data-request list. Never invent batch data, method capability, limits, validation status, acceptance criteria, or regulatory agreement.

## Numerical and applicability firewall

Before adopting any design element from a worked example, ask:

- Is the product type, process physics, unit operation, scale, equipment, material behavior, sampling unit, and data hierarchy comparable?
- Is the example number a requirement, an illustrative design, or merely the outcome of one company's risk decision?
- Are the example's assumptions, prior knowledge, variability estimates, statistical power, confidence/coverage target, and acceptance criteria available and valid here?
- Does the proposed design comply with current regional requirements and approved dossier commitments?
- Would the conclusion remain defensible if the example were removed and only the project's own evidence remained?

Specific batch counts, sample counts, scale percentages, confidence/coverage levels, capability thresholds, control-limit establishment points, study durations, and stability schedules from the examples are non-binding case facts. They may be considered as options only after independent scientific, statistical, quality, and regulatory justification.
